The list before the ledger

What Does a Chart of Accounts Look Like for a Trading Business

The tax form is already a chart of accounts. It has 25 expense lines, a solo trading desk fills about 13 of them, and the cost that matters most to a trader is not on it at all.

By Rex  ·  XAUUSD  ·  10 min read

Ask what does a chart of accounts look like and most answers show you a corporate ledger with four hundred lines, which is both accurate and completely unusable for a one person trading desk. A chart of accounts is just the list of labelled buckets your money moves through, and the useful version is short. What follows is a working one for a trading business, built from a list the tax authority has already written for you, plus the reason most traders quietly need one.

What does a chart of accounts look like when someone else has written it

Start from the version you are obliged to produce anyway. In the United States a sole proprietor reports business profit on Schedule C of Form 1040, and Part II of that form is a fixed list of expense categories. It is a chart of accounts. Somebody else designed it, it is free, and your accountant will ask you for it in that shape regardless of what you keep during the year.

I counted the lines on the 2026 form, published as a PDF on irs.gov. Part II runs from line 8 to line 27b and contains 25 expense lines, because three of the numbered lines split into lettered parts:

  • Line 16 splits into 16a mortgage interest paid to banks, 16b vehicle loan interest, and 16c other interest
  • Line 20 splits into 20a rent or lease of vehicles, machinery and equipment, and 20b other business property
  • Line 24 splits into 24a travel and 24b deductible meals
  • Line 27b is not a category at all, it is the total of Part V, which the form describes as business expenses not included on lines 8 to 27a

That last one is the design lesson. The form has 24 named categories and one deliberate overflow bucket at the end, with a schedule behind it for listing what went in. Every workable chart of accounts has that shape: a short list of things you expect, plus one honest place for things you did not.

Part II also carries an instruction worth copying, printed in the heading itself: enter expenses for business use of your home only on line 30. In other words, one cost gets its own treatment and is deliberately kept out of the main list to stop it being counted twice. Your own chart will need one or two rules like that.

Roughly half the official list does not apply to you

Here is the part that makes the form useful as a starting point rather than as a finished answer. Of those 25 lines, a solo screen-based trading business plausibly fills about 13, and about 12 stay empty year after year. That split is my classification rather than an IRS category, and your business may divide them differently, but the direction is not controversial.

The ones that usually stay blank: car and truck expenses, contract labor, depletion, employee benefit programs, mortgage interest, vehicle loan interest, pension and profit-sharing plans, rent of vehicles and machinery, repairs and maintenance, supplies, wages, and the energy efficient commercial buildings deduction. A trader with no staff, no premises and no inventory is not going to touch most of that.

The ones that do get used: advertising, commissions and fees, depreciation, insurance, other interest, legal and professional services, office expense, other business property rent, taxes and licenses, travel, deductible meals, utilities, and the overflow line. That is a real chart of accounts, and it is thirteen items long.

Which tells you something about the corporate charts you find when you search this question. They are long because they describe businesses with inventory, payroll and premises. Copying one wholesale gives you dozens of accounts that will never hold a number, and an empty account is not harmless. It is a place for a cost to be filed and then never looked at again.

A working chart of accounts for a trading business

Below is the version I would actually build. The numbering is the standard convention, blocks of a thousand by account type, which exists so that you can add an account later without renumbering everything and so that sorting by number groups the statement for you.

BlockTypeAccounts a trading desk actually uses
1000Assets1010 business bank account · 1020 broker account equity · 1030 broker account held at second firm · 1100 equipment · 1110 accumulated depreciation
2000Liabilities2010 credit card · 2020 tax set aside · 2030 accrued accountant fee
3000Equity3010 owner capital introduced · 3020 owner drawings · 3030 retained earnings
4000Income4010 realised trading gains · 4020 realised trading losses · 4030 interest received on cash · 4040 rebates received
5000Direct trading costs5010 commission · 5020 spread cost · 5030 swap and financing · 5040 market data fees · 5050 slippage allowance
6000Operating expenses6010 platform and software · 6020 internet and phone · 6030 accountant and legal · 6040 training and books · 6050 equipment under threshold · 6060 bank charges · 6090 other

Three choices in that table are worth defending, because they are the ones people get wrong.

Realised gains and losses are two accounts, not one net account. If you net them you lose the ability to see gross activity, and the gross number is what tells you whether a flat month was quiet or violent. The net is a subtraction you can always perform later. The detail is not recoverable once you have thrown it away.

Direct trading costs get their own block, separate from operating expenses. Commission, spread, financing and data scale with how much you trade. Your accountant's fee does not. Keeping them in separate blocks is what lets you answer the only cost question that matters in a bad month, which is how much of this was the market and how much was the overhead. That is the same fixed and variable split set out in fixed and variable costs of a trading business.

There is a 6090 other, and it should stay small. Copy the form's overflow bucket, and then treat its size as a diagnostic. If more than a few per cent of spending lands in other, the chart is missing an account that your business actually has, and the fix is to add that account rather than to widen the bucket.

Slippage is the account nobody opens

Account 5050 is the one that separates a trading business's books from a generic small business template, and it is the reason to build the chart yourself rather than download one.

Commission appears on your statement. Spread is embedded in your fill. Financing arrives overnight. Slippage, the difference between the price you expected and the price you got, appears nowhere as a line item, because from the broker's point of view nothing happened: you were filled at the market. It is a real cost paid in full and invoiced by nobody.

You cannot book it from a statement, so you book it from your own records: expected price against filled price, every trade, in whatever unit you work in. That makes it an estimate rather than a reconciled figure, and it should be labelled as one in your books. An estimate you can see beats a real cost you cannot. The broker side of this reconciliation is in how to reconcile your broker statement.

The general rule behind that account: if a cost is real and recurring, it gets a line, even if nobody sends you a bill for it. Costs without invoices are exactly the ones that go unmanaged.

What the chart does once it exists

A chart of accounts is infrastructure, so its value shows up in what other things become possible.

It makes the monthly review mechanical instead of a memory exercise. You are reading a sorted list rather than reconstructing the month, which is the difference described in your monthly profit and loss review.

It makes the budget checkable. A budget built on categories that do not match your ledger cannot be compared with the outcome, so the comparison silently stops happening. Same categories, same names, and the business budget spreadsheet becomes a document you can mark against reality.

It decides where a cost lands in time, which is a separate question from which account it belongs in, and the one handled in cash basis vs accrual accounting. The chart answers where. The method answers when. You need both, and confusing them is how a year end stops balancing.

And it makes the question of whether a loss is an expense answerable rather than philosophical, which I have set out separately in is a losing trade a business expense. In the chart above a realised loss sits in the 4000 block, with income, because it is a trading outcome and not an operating cost. Putting it in the 5000 block with commission would make your cost of trading look like it moves with your results, which it does not.

Build it in fifteen minutes

  1. Open the form. Schedule C Part II, or your own jurisdiction's equivalent return. Someone has already written the list you will be asked for.
  2. Strike out what will never apply. On the worked count above that removed 12 of 25 lines. Deleting them now is what keeps the chart readable later.
  3. Add the accounts the form has no idea about. Commission, spread, swap, data, slippage. A generic form has no concept of a cost of trading, which is most of your variable cost.
  4. Number them in blocks of a thousand by type. Assets 1000, liabilities 2000, equity 3000, income 4000, direct costs 5000, operating expenses 6000, with gaps between account numbers so there is room to insert.
  5. Write one sentence per account saying what goes in it. This is the step people skip, and it is the one that prevents the same cost landing in two different accounts in March and September.
  6. Leave one other account, and watch its size. A growing overflow bucket is the chart telling you it is missing a line.

Fifteen minutes, and it then stays stable for years. The chart is the part of the books you should expect to change least.

Frequently asked questions

What does a chart of accounts look like for a one person trading business?

Short. Around thirty accounts in six numbered blocks: assets, liabilities, equity, income, direct trading costs and operating expenses. The table above is a working version. The main difference from a generic small business chart is a dedicated block for commission, spread, financing, data and slippage, which a standard template does not contain because most businesses have no cost of trading.

Do I have to use account numbers?

No, but they pay for themselves quickly. Numbering in blocks of a thousand by type means a report sorted by account number is already grouped the way a profit and loss statement is, and leaving gaps means adding an account later does not force you to renumber anything. If you are keeping the books in a spreadsheet, the numbers also make sorting and lookup formulas reliable.

How many accounts is too many?

When you start hesitating over which account a routine cost belongs in, you have too many. Two accounts that could plausibly hold the same receipt will each hold some of them, and neither total will be right. Merge them. The opposite failure, too few accounts, shows up as a large other balance at year end.

Where do trading losses go?

In the chart above, in the income block alongside realised gains, because a loss is a trading outcome rather than a cost of running the business. Keeping gains and losses in separate accounts rather than one net figure preserves the gross activity, which is what tells you whether a flat month was quiet or busy. How that is treated for tax is a separate question and a matter for a qualified professional in your jurisdiction.

Is the Schedule C list relevant if I am not in the United States?

The specific lines are US specific, but the method transfers: find the expense categories on the return you are actually required to file, delete the ones that will never apply to you, then add the trading costs the form does not know exist. Starting from the shape your filing will take is what stops the year end being a reconstruction exercise.
