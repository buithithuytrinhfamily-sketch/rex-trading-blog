One division, and what it refuses to tell you

What Is Working Capital Ratio in Accounting

Current assets over current liabilities, and the answer is read wrong in both directions. Apple closed its 2026 year at 1.02 to 1. A worked trading business sits at 2.29 and is still one unrecorded tax bill from trouble.

By Rex  ·  XAUUSD  ·  10 min read

The short answer to what is working capital ratio in accounting is one division: current assets divided by current liabilities. That is it. The useful answer takes a little longer, because the number it produces is routinely misread in both directions. A trading business can post a ratio above 2 and still be one bad month from trouble, and Apple closed its 2026 financial year with a ratio of 1.02 to 1. If a high number meant safe and a low number meant fragile, those two facts would not sit together.

What is working capital ratio in accounting, stated as a formula

Current assets divided by current liabilities. Both terms carry the word current, and current has a precise meaning: it is anything that converts to cash, or has to be paid, inside twelve months. That twelve month line is the whole of the idea. Everything on both sides of this ratio is near term, which is why it answers a near term question.

Notice how this differs from working capital itself, which is current assets minus current liabilities. That subtraction gives you an amount. This division gives you a proportion. The amount tells you how much slack you have, and the proportion tells you how much slack you have relative to what is coming due. They answer different questions and you want both, which is why the relationship between the amount and your net worth is worked through separately in is working capital just equity.

The reason the ratio exists at all is that an amount means nothing without a scale beside it. Slack of 5,000 is comfortable against bills of 2,000 and frightening against bills of 50,000. Dividing removes the size of the business from the picture so two sets of books can be compared at all.

The worked books, line by line

Here is a one person trading business. Every figure below is assumed, chosen to be ordinary rather than flattering, and nothing here reflects any real account.

What it owns, due or usable within twelve months. Cash at the broker, 6,000. Cash in the business bank account, 4,000. Data and platform fees paid in advance, 300. Current assets total 10,300.

What it owes within twelve months. Tax set aside but not yet paid, 2,800. Platform and data fees payable, 200. Business credit card balance, 1,500. Current liabilities total 4,500.

Divide: 10,300 over 4,500 gives 2.29 to 1. Subtract instead and working capital is 5,800. Both numbers describe the same month. The first says there is a little over twice as much near term money as near term obligation. The second says the cushion is 5,800 in actual currency.

On its own, 2.29 reads as comfortable. Hold that thought, because the next section is where most readers change their mind about what comfortable means.

Why a famous company runs a ratio near 1.00

Apple Inc filed its Form 10-K for the financial year ended 26 September 2026. The audited figures show current assets of 173,857 million dollars against current liabilities of 169,915 million dollars. Working capital is 3,942 million dollars, which sounds enormous, and the ratio is 1.0232, which sounds thin. Both come from the same two lines.

Nobody seriously argues that Apple is a fragile business. What the low ratio reflects is a company that converts stock into cash quickly, collects reliably, and sees no reason to leave idle cash sitting in current assets to flatter a number. A ratio near 1 in that context is efficiency, not distress.

The ratio does not know what business it is describing. It measures cover, not safety, and the two are only the same thing when the incoming cash is dependable.

That is the sentence to carry away. A supermarket can live near 1.0 because tomorrow's takings are close to certain. A trading business cannot, because its incoming cash is not a receivable from a customer who owes it. It is a result that has not happened yet, and might not. The less predictable the inflow, the more cover you need for the same level of comfort, which means the right number for you is not the right number for Apple, and no textbook figure substitutes for knowing which of those two situations you are in.

What moves the ratio in a trading business

Two stresses, applied to the same worked books, make the mechanics obvious.

Stress A, the tax money gets traded. The 2,800 set aside for tax is sitting in the broker account, and it is lost. Current assets fall to 7,500, liabilities are unchanged at 4,500, and the ratio drops to 1.67. The liability did not move, because losing the money does not cancel the tax. This is the single most common way a solvent looking trading business becomes an insolvent one, and it is the reason the timing rule in cash basis vs accrual accounting matters more here than it does in most small businesses.

Stress B, an ordinary losing month. A 20 percent loss on the 6,000 broker balance takes 1,200. Current assets fall to 9,100 and the ratio moves to 2.02. Uncomfortable, survivable, and notably less damaging than stress A, even though the amounts are not far apart. The difference is that stress A spent money that was already promised to somebody else.

And the figure worth knowing before either happens: starting from 2.29, this business reaches a ratio of exactly 1.00 after losing 5,800 of current assets, which is 96.7 percent of the entire broker balance. That is the real meaning of the cushion. Not a feeling, a distance, and one you can measure at the end of any month.

The trap that is specific to traders

In most small businesses the current liabilities are visible. An invoice arrives, a bill is dated, somebody chases you. The trading equivalent is quieter, because the largest near term liability is usually tax on gains already made, and nobody invoices you for it in the month it accrues.

So it does not feel like a liability. It feels like balance. It is sitting in the broker account looking exactly like trading capital, and it is spendable, and that is precisely the problem. A ratio computed with that money counted only as an asset, while the matching liability goes unrecorded, will read far too high. Record both sides and the ratio tells the truth. Record one side and it flatters you right up until the bill lands.

This is why the ratio is worth computing from a real chart of accounts rather than from memory. The structure that keeps the two sides separate is laid out in what a chart of accounts looks like, and the equity side of the same statement is in what balance sheet equity is.

What the ratio cannot tell you

Three limits, and each has caught somebody out.

It does not look inside current assets. Cash and a prepaid subscription both count, and they are not equally useful when a bill arrives. Prepaid fees cannot pay a tax demand. A ratio of 2.0 built mostly from prepayments is weaker than a ratio of 1.5 built from cash.

It is a photograph, not a film. It describes one date. A business can be comfortable on the last day of the month and thin on the fifteenth, and the ratio will never show it. That is what a cash reserve is for, and the sizing of one is in how to build a cash reserve for your trading business.

A higher number is not automatically better. A very high ratio can mean idle cash doing nothing, which is its own kind of waste. Apple at 1.02 is making a deliberate choice, not a mistake.

How to use it at your next monthly review

Compute it once a month, on the same day each month, and write it down next to the previous months. One reading tells you almost nothing. A line of readings tells you the direction, and direction is the part you can act on.

Then ask the only two questions that matter. First, is every near term obligation actually recorded, with tax at the top of that list. Second, is the trend over the last three months going up, flat, or down. A business drifting from 2.3 to 1.9 to 1.5 has a problem that no single month's number would have revealed, and it has that problem while still looking adequate on paper. The month to act is the second one, not the one where the ratio finally dips below 1. The place to record all of this is the one page trading business plan.

Frequently asked questions

What is working capital ratio in accounting, in one sentence?

It is current assets divided by current liabilities, where current means convertible to cash or payable within twelve months. It expresses how many times over your near term resources cover your near term obligations.

Is it the same as the current ratio?

Yes. Working capital ratio and current ratio are two names for the same division. Working capital on its own, with no ratio in the name, is the subtraction instead, current assets minus current liabilities, and that one is an amount rather than a proportion.

What is a good working capital ratio?

There is no universal figure, and anyone quoting one without asking what the business does is guessing. What matters is whether your incoming cash is predictable. Apple runs at 1.02 with highly predictable collections. A business whose income is uncertain needs more cover for the same comfort, and the honest answer is that you set the number against your own obligations rather than copying it.

Should the cash in my broker account count as a current asset?

If you can withdraw it within twelve months, yes. The mistake is not counting it as an asset, it is forgetting to record the tax and fees it is already promised to, which sit on the other side of the same ratio.

Can the ratio look fine while the business is in trouble?

Easily. The two usual causes are unrecorded liabilities, tax being the common one, and current assets that are technically current but cannot actually pay a bill, such as prepaid subscriptions.

How often should I calculate it?

Monthly, on a fixed date, kept as a series. The trend carries the information. No entry, stop or target discussed should be treated as a signal, and this is bookkeeping rather than a trading method.

Where this leaves you

Asking what is working capital ratio in accounting gets you a formula in about four seconds. What takes longer is accepting that the formula answers a narrower question than most people want it to. It tells you how well near term resources cover near term obligations on one particular day. It does not tell you whether the business is sound, whether the month was good, or whether the number is high enough, because that last answer depends entirely on how reliable your incoming cash is.

For a trading business the practical work is almost never the division. It is making sure both sides of it are honestly recorded, and watching the direction over several months rather than reacting to one reading.
