A range is an operating problem, not an analytical one

Trading Sideways Market Conditions, and the Cost of Filling the Time

Costs are charged on the distance price travels, not on where it ends up. The Fed schedules 8 decisions in 2026, roughly 31.5 trading days apart, and the trades taken to fill those gaps can cost more than the planned ones earn.

By Rex  ·  XAUUSD  ·  10 min read

Most of what gets written about trading sideways market conditions treats the range as a puzzle to be solved, as though the right indicator would reveal which way it breaks. That is the wrong department. A range is not an analytical problem, it is an operating problem, and it is the condition under which a trading business pays full costs for no movement. The question an operator asks is not how to trade the range. It is what the range costs to sit through, what it costs to fill, and which of those two bills is larger.

What a sideways market actually is, in operating terms

Strip out the chart language and a range is a period in which price travels a long way and arrives nowhere. The bars still move. The distance covered is real. What is missing is net displacement, and net displacement is the only thing a directional position gets paid for.

That gap between distance and displacement is the entire issue, because your costs are charged on distance. Every round turn costs the same whether it was part of a move that went somewhere or part of a move that came straight back. The market does not discount the spread because the week was boring.

So a range is not a neutral period. It is a period with the revenue line suppressed and the cost line unchanged. On any other business's accounts you would recognise that shape immediately, and you would not respond to it by increasing the number of transactions.

Trading sideways market conditions is a cost problem first

Here is where the discipline usually fails. Faced with a flat week, almost nobody reduces activity. They increase it, because the absence of a good setup feels like a reason to look harder rather than a reason to look less. The screen is open, the day is allocated to trading, and an empty day feels like a wasted one.

That instinct is the expensive part, and it is worth putting a number on it rather than a warning.

Take a business that risks a fixed 100 dollars per trade and pays a round turn cost, spread plus commission, of 8 dollars. I am using those two figures throughout, and you should swap in your own. The cost is therefore 8 divided by 100, which is 8 per cent of one risk unit per trade, before the trade is right or wrong about anything.

Now suppose the plan calls for 6 trades in a month, and a range month produces 18 because the operator kept looking. That is 12 extra trades. At 8 dollars each the extra activity costs 96 dollars, which is 0.96 of a full risk unit. In other words, a month of forcing trades in a flat market loses very nearly one whole losing trade's worth of capital in costs alone, with no directional loss required at all.

Carry that for a year and it is 144 extra trades, 1,152 dollars, or 11.5 risk units handed over for activity that the market was never going to pay for.

The number that settles the argument

There is one more calculation and it is the one that should end the debate internally.

Suppose the business genuinely has an edge, and its 6 planned trades each carry an expectancy of plus 0.10 risk units. That is a real and respectable edge. Those 6 trades earn 0.60 risk units of gross expectancy in the month.

The 12 forced trades, by assumption, have no edge, because they were taken in conditions the method was not built for. They cost 0.96 risk units. That is 160 per cent of the gross expectancy the good trades produced.

Read that again, because it is not a rhetorical flourish. Under these assumptions the filler trades cost more than the entire edge of the real ones. A business with a working method posts a losing month, and the method was never the problem. The schedule was.

For those extra trades to be worth taking at all, each one would need an expectancy better than 8 per cent of a risk unit just to cover its own cost. If you believed your range trades cleared that bar you would have put them in the plan.

How long the waiting actually lasts

Operators handle a known quiet period better than an open ended one, so it is worth knowing roughly how long these stretches run.

Gold spends a great deal of its time waiting for scheduled macro catalysts, and the largest of those are on a published calendar. The Federal Reserve lists 8 scheduled Federal Open Market Committee meetings for 2026, of which 4 carry projection materials. That is the full schedule, and I counted it off the calendar rather than from memory.

Assume roughly 252 trading days in a year. Divide by 8 and the average gap between scheduled Fed decisions is about 31.5 trading days, call it six working weeks. The economic calendar has plenty else on it, but the point stands: the biggest scheduled movers are spaced about six weeks apart, and the intervals between them are not an anomaly to be traded through. They are the normal state of the business.

A firm that only functions in the fortnight around a Fed decision is not a firm with a bad strategy. It is a firm that has not budgeted for its own calendar.

What the inbox says about this

I went through the messages in my inbox that mention overtrading, roughly 16 of them. That is a keyword count across the messages I have received, not a survey, and it is not evidence of anything beyond what people choose to write to me about. But the language is consistent in a way worth repeating.

One reader wrote: "I used to overtrade. I opened too many positions, and most of them lacked logic and were driven by FOMO. That's why my account never grew."

Another: "Before, after a losing trade, I would often try to make it back immediately. I was taking too many trades, using bigger lot sizes, and sometimes entering without a proper setup."

A third described being disciplined by the rules of a funded programme rather than by himself: "they penalized me for rapid fire trading, for a couple of days. I was opening too many trades. I had to adjust the number of orders."

And one asked the question plainly: "isn't it considered overtrading to have more than 8 trades per day?"

Notice what nobody writes. Nobody writes to say the range was unreadable. They write to say they took trades that lacked logic, that they entered without a proper setup, that they opened too many positions. The complaint is never about the market being hard to analyse. It is always about activity that was not supposed to happen.

The turnover evidence

This is not a new finding, and it has been measured in a far larger sample than my inbox.

Barber and Odean studied 66,465 households at a large discount broker between 1991 and 1996 and published the results in the Journal of Finance. The households that traded most earned an annual return of 11.4 per cent over the period, while the market returned 17.9 per cent. The average household earned 16.4 per cent and turned over 75 per cent of its portfolio in a year.

The gap between the heaviest traders and the market is 6.5 percentage points a year. These are equity investors rather than gold traders, and the mechanism is not identical, but the finding transfers cleanly enough: higher turnover produced lower net returns in a large sample over a long period. Activity was not the thing being rewarded.

Their own conclusion was blunter than mine, and they put it in the title of the paper.

What an operator does with this

Write the trade count into the plan, not just the risk. Most plans cap loss per trade and loss per day and say nothing at all about how many trades the month is allowed to contain. A maximum trade count is the only limit that directly addresses this failure, and it is the one almost nobody writes down. Put it next to the risk ceiling in the one page trading business plan template.

Price your own inactivity. Work out your actual round turn cost once, divide it by your risk unit, and keep the percentage somewhere visible. When that figure is 8 per cent, you know immediately that twelve unplanned trades cost most of a losing trade. An abstract instruction to avoid overtrading is easy to ignore. A number is harder.

Separate the stop for the day from the stop for the conditions. A daily loss limit stops a bad day from becoming a disaster, but it does nothing about a flat month, because the forced trades mostly scratch rather than lose. You need a second rule that stops trading when the conditions the method needs are absent, which is a different trigger entirely from knowing when to stop trading on a losing run.

Count the scratches in the review. Trades that finish near flat disappear from a profit and loss summary and are the exact trades this article is about. Track trade count and total costs as their own line items, alongside the other metrics a trading business should track. If the month's cost line rose while the risk taken stayed flat, the range got you, whatever the bottom line says.

Give the quiet period a job. The alternative to trading a range is not sitting and watching it. Review, record keeping, strategy testing and admin all have to happen sometime, and a flat market is when they are cheapest to do.

Frequently asked questions

Is trading sideways market conditions always a mistake?

No. Some methods are built for ranges and have the statistics to show it. The mistake is trading a range with a method built for trends, which is what happens when activity is driven by the calendar rather than by the conditions the method needs.

How do I know whether my range trades actually have an edge?

Tag them separately in your records and compare their expectancy with your planned trades. At a round turn cost of 8 per cent of a risk unit, a range trade needs an expectancy above that just to pay for itself. Most people have never separated the two populations, so the question has never been answered.

What does a sideways market cost if I simply do nothing?

Almost nothing, which is the point. Sitting out costs you the opportunity, any platform or data subscription you pay monthly, and nothing else. Filling the time costs you the round turn on every trade you added.

How long do these periods usually last?

There is no fixed answer, but the scheduled calendar gives you a sense of scale. The Fed holds 8 scheduled meetings a year, about 31.5 trading days apart on average, so stretches of several weeks without a large scheduled catalyst are normal rather than unusual.

Should I switch to a lower timeframe to find movement?

Be careful with that one. Dropping timeframe increases trade frequency, and trade frequency is the term in the cost equation you were trying to control. You would be solving a revenue problem by increasing the cost line.
