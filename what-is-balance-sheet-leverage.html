What Is Balance Sheet Leverage

Total assets divided by equity, and your trading account already has a value for it. At the ESMA gold cap of 20 to 1, a 10,000 account carrying 200,000 runs at 20 times, which is 80 per cent of the 25 times ceiling the Federal Reserve sets for banks.

By Rex  ·  XAUUSD  ·  11 min read

Ask what is balance sheet leverage and you get an accounting answer that sounds like it belongs to somebody else: total assets divided by equity, a ratio for banks and listed companies with real balance sheets. It is not somebody else's number. If you trade a leveraged account, you are running a balance sheet whether you ever write one down, and that ratio already has a value today. Most traders have never calculated it, and quite a few would change what they are doing if they did, because the number is usually larger than the mental picture that goes with it.

What is balance sheet leverage, in one division

Balance sheet leverage is total assets divided by equity. That is the whole definition. Equity is the part of the business that belongs to you. Assets are everything the business is carrying, including the part funded by somebody else. Divide the second by the first and you get a multiple, and that multiple tells you how much is riding on a given percentage move in the value of what you hold.

The reason it matters is not the ratio itself but what it does to percentages. If your assets are four times your equity, a one per cent fall in the value of those assets is a four per cent fall in your equity. The move in the market is unchanged. The move in your business is multiplied, and the multiplier is exactly this number.

If the equity half of that sentence is doing unfamiliar work, it is worth being precise about it first, which is the subject of what balance sheet equity is, and the related confusion between equity and working capital is sorted out in is working capital just equity.

Your account has a balance sheet whether you write one or not

Here is the translation, and it is simpler than people expect.

Your equity is the cash in the account. Your assets are the positions you are carrying, valued at what they control rather than at what you put down for them. The gap between the two is funded by the broker, and whether you call that a loan, margin or exposure, it behaves like the liability side of a balance sheet: it magnifies the result and it has to be serviced.

Take an account with 10,000 of equity carrying a position with a notional value of 200,000. Assets 200,000, equity 10,000, balance sheet leverage 20 times. Your equity is 5 per cent of the assets you are carrying, and the other 95 per cent, 190,000 of it, is not yours. Expressed the way a lender would, that is a debt to equity ratio of 19 to 1.

Nothing in that paragraph is a judgement. It is a division, and it is a division you can do on your own account in about ten seconds. The point of doing it is that it converts a setting in a platform into a sentence about your business, and the sentence is harder to ignore. Roughly 73 messages in my inbox mention leverage or margin, which is a keyword count rather than a survey, and the way people talk about it is almost always as a platform setting. One reader asked: "Hey, can you please tell me what's this leverage meaning and what size of leverage I should choose here?" Another: "What do you suggest? I set the leverage to 100, what do you think?"

The question they are asking is a balance sheet question, but it does not look like one while it is sitting in a dropdown menu.

What the people who regulate this for a living put the number at

You do not have to invent a benchmark. Two sets of regulators have already published theirs, for two very different kinds of business, and the comparison is useful precisely because neither of them was thinking about you.

Start with banks. The Federal Reserve's capital rule includes a minimum leverage ratio of 4 per cent for all banking organisations, measured as tier 1 capital against total assets. Turn that the other way up and 4 per cent is one twenty fifth, so a bank is required to keep its assets at or below 25 times its capital. That is the ceiling for institutions with risk departments, funding lines and regulators reading their books.

Now the retail side. ESMA set leverage caps for retail contracts for difference at 30 to 1 for major currency pairs, 20 to 1 for non major currency pairs, gold and major equity indices, 10 to 1 for commodities other than gold, 5 to 1 for individual equities, and 2 to 1 for cryptocurrencies. Gold sits in the 20 to 1 tier, and ESMA was explicit that gold got the gentler treatment because it has shown lower historic price volatility than other commodities.

Put the two beside each other. A retail trader at the gold cap runs balance sheet leverage of 20 times. A bank is not permitted above 25 times. The retail account is at 80 per cent of the ceiling set for regulated banks, without the risk department, the funding lines or the diversification.

That comparison is not an argument that 20 times is wrong. Banks and traders carry entirely different assets for entirely different reasons. It is an argument that the number deserves to be chosen on purpose, because it is sitting in territory that serious institutions treat as an outer limit.

The move that takes the account

The clearest way to feel a leverage ratio is to ask what percentage move wipes you out, and that is a one line calculation: it is the reciprocal of the ratio.

At 20 times, on 10,000 of equity carrying 200,000 of assets, a 1 per cent adverse move is 2,000, which is 20 per cent of the equity. A 2.5 per cent move is 5,000, half the account. A 5 per cent move is 10,000, all of it. The market has moved 5 per cent. The business has moved 100 per cent.

ESMA's package included a margin close out rule set at 50 per cent of initial required margin, per account, so in this example the close out point sits at that 2.5 per cent move. That rule exists to stop the equity being eroded all the way to nothing, which is a genuine protection, and it is also a reminder of how small a market move the arrangement is built to survive.

Run the same arithmetic at other levels and the picture is the whole story of leverage in one column. Cash only, 1 times, it takes a 100 per cent move. At 10 times, 10 per cent. At 20 times, 5 per cent. At 30 times, 3.3 per cent. Each step up the ladder is a step down in how much ordinary market noise your business can absorb, and the relationship is not gentle, it is a reciprocal. The longer run consequence of sitting at the wrong end of that curve is the subject of risk of ruin in trading.

Two ways to cut the number, and only one of them is free

Because the ratio is a fraction, there are exactly two levers: make the top smaller or make the bottom bigger.

Carrying a smaller position cuts assets and the ratio falls immediately. It costs nothing but ambition, and it is the lever available today, which is the practical content of position sizing for gold trading and of the familiar ceiling discussed in the 1 per cent rule.

Adding equity cuts the ratio too, and the arithmetic is worth seeing because it is less intuitive. Hold the position at 200,000 and add capital: at 10,000 of equity it is 20 times and a 5 per cent move ends it. Add 10,000 and it is 10 times, needing a 10 per cent move. Add 30,000 and it is 5 times, needing 20 per cent. Reach 100,000 of equity and it is 2 times, needing a 50 per cent move. The position never changed. Only the denominator did.

That second lever is the one most retail traders have backwards. The instinct after a loss is to keep the equity where it is and reach for more exposure, which raises the ratio precisely when the business is least able to absorb a move. One reader described doing exactly that: "My account initially had leverage at 1:50, then when I started losing I got it to 1:500 to open bigger positions, but I went really bad as you can see." Another wrote: "I'm considering adding funds to avoid margin call." Those two sentences are the same lever pulled in opposite directions, and the second one at least moves the ratio the right way, even if the better moment for it was before the position was on.

What a business owner does with this number

Treat it as a reported figure rather than a feeling. Three habits, none of them dramatic.

Calculate it, in writing, at the point you open a position, not afterwards. Notional value of everything you are carrying, divided by the cash in the account. One number, written down.

Set a ceiling for it in advance and put it on the same page as your other limits, next to your daily loss limit. A ceiling chosen while nothing is happening is a policy. A ceiling chosen while a position is moving is a reaction, and reactions are what the ratio punishes hardest.

Review it as a trend rather than a snapshot. The useful question at the end of a month is not what your leverage was on a given afternoon but whether it has been drifting upward, because drift is the normal way businesses end up overextended. Nobody decides to double their leverage. They take slightly larger positions for eleven weeks. The monthly review habit it belongs in is set out in the break even point of your trading business.

What this article will not do is tell you what your ratio should be. That depends on the volatility of what you hold, your method, your other income and how much of a drawdown your actual life can absorb, and anybody handing you a single right answer without knowing those things is guessing with your money. The number is diagnostic, not prescriptive. Knowing it is the part that is not optional.

Frequently asked questions

What is balance sheet leverage, in one sentence?

Total assets divided by equity, which for a trading account means the notional value of the positions you are carrying divided by the cash in the account, and it tells you how much a one per cent move in the market becomes once it reaches your equity.

Is balance sheet leverage the same as the leverage my broker offers?

They are closely related but not identical. Broker leverage is the maximum the platform will let you use. Balance sheet leverage is what you are actually using right now. An account set to 1:500 that is carrying nothing has broker leverage of 500 and balance sheet leverage of zero, and an account set to 1:30 carrying its maximum has balance sheet leverage of 30. The setting is permission, the ratio is behaviour.

What is a normal level?

There is no universal normal, which is why the published benchmarks are useful instead. The Federal Reserve requires banking organisations to hold tier 1 capital of at least 4 per cent of total assets, implying a ceiling of 25 times. ESMA caps retail gold contracts for difference at 20 to 1 and major currency pairs at 30 to 1. Those are limits set by regulators for particular purposes, not recommendations for your account.

How do I work mine out?

Add up the notional value of every open position, which is contract size times number of contracts times price, not the margin you posted. Divide by the current cash equity in the account. If you hold positions in more than one account, do it per account and then once across all of them, because the combined figure is the one that describes your business.

Does a stop loss reduce my balance sheet leverage?

No. A stop is an instruction about when to exit, and it limits the loss you intend to take. It does not change the size of the assets you are carrying, so the ratio is the same with or without it. The two tools answer different questions, and a stop can also be passed over in fast conditions, which is exactly when a high ratio matters most.

Why compare a trading account to a bank at all?

Because banks are the most heavily studied leveraged businesses in existence and their limits are published. The comparison is not meant to suggest the two are alike. It gives you an external reference point for a number that otherwise gets judged purely by how it feels.
